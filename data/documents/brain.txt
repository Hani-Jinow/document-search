The human brain is one of the most complex organs in the body. Although it represents only about 2% of a person's body weight, it consumes roughly 20% of the body's energy. The brain runs on approximately 20 watts of power, which is about the same amount of energy required to power a dim LED light bulb.

The brain contains approximately 86 billion neurons. Neurons are specialized cells that transmit information through electrical and chemical signals. These neurons communicate through connections called synapses, and the total number of synaptic connections is estimated to be in the hundreds of trillions. This vast network allows humans to think, learn, remember, and make decisions.

Different regions of the brain perform different functions. The frontal lobe is associated with planning, reasoning, decision-making, and personality. The temporal lobe plays an important role in hearing and memory. The parietal lobe helps process sensory information such as touch and temperature, while the occipital lobe is primarily responsible for vision.

Despite popular myths, humans do not use only 10% of their brains. Brain imaging studies show that most regions of the brain are active at different times depending on the task being performed. Even simple activities such as walking, speaking, or reading involve multiple areas working together.

The brain is protected by the skull and surrounded by cerebrospinal fluid, which acts as a cushion against physical impacts. It also receives a constant supply of oxygen and nutrients through an extensive network of blood vessels. Because brain cells require a continuous energy supply, interruptions in blood flow can quickly lead to damage.

One remarkable feature of the brain is neuroplasticity. Neuroplasticity refers to the brain's ability to adapt and reorganize itself throughout life. When people learn new skills, practice an activity, or recover from certain injuries, the brain can strengthen existing neural pathways or create new ones. This ability plays a key role in learning, memory, and recovery after injury.

Scientists continue to study the brain to better understand intelligence, consciousness, emotions, and neurological disorders. Advances in neuroscience have helped improve treatments for conditions such as Alzheimer's disease, Parkinson's disease, epilepsy, and stroke. Despite significant progress, many aspects of the brain remain a mystery, making it one of the most fascinating areas of scientific research.
